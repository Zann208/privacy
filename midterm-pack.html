One-box formula sheet

Professor Discussion Questions — Ready Answers

These are direct lecture-style discussion prompts or close equivalents from the supplied slides.

Topic 1

Security/privacy property questions

Q: A ransomware attacker hijacks a computer, encrypts files and demands ransom. Which property is breached?

Exam answer: The lecture places ransomware under confidentiality. It can also make data unavailable to authorised users, so availability is affected too. If forced to choose the slide’s intended single property, use confidentiality.

Q: A malicious employee sells company secrets. Which property?

Confidentiality. Secret information is disclosed to an unauthorised party.

Q: A person is re-identified from a publicly available anonymised dataset. Which property?

Indistinguishability is the direct lecture answer: the individual could be singled out/distinguished. Re-identification may also compromise anonymity/confidentiality depending on context.

Q: Which are sensitive/confidential: social networking data, location data, transaction data, web search history?

All four.

Q: Which techniques preserve integrity: hashing, encryption, logs, backup?

Hashing and logs.

Q: Why are humans a significant vulnerability?

Lack of cybersecurity awareness, susceptibility to social engineering/phishing, weak passwords, clicking malicious links, and malicious insiders. Mitigate with education, strong authentication, technological controls and vigilant human behaviour.
Topic 2

Quantitative privacy questions

Q: p(apple)=0.6 and p(orange)=0.4. What is entropy?

H(X)=−0.6log₂0.6−0.4log₂0.4=0.971 bits

Q: Which Alejandra threat model gives highest risk?

Threat Model 3, because the adversary knows the combination of postcode + medical code. The lecture concludes that combining attributes makes patterns more unique and gives the adversary more information.

Q: Why does TM2 have higher entropy / less privacy than TM1?

TM2 medical-code probabilities (0.5, 0.4, 0.1) are more evenly distributed than TM1 postcode probabilities (0.3, 0.6, 0.1), creating greater uncertainty/new information in the lecture’s entropy interpretation. Therefore TM2 has higher entropy and less privacy than TM1.
Topic 3

Model/attack questions

Q: Which de-identification technique is vulnerable to homogeneity attacks?

k-anonymity.

Q: Does l-diversity provide sufficient privacy against every inference attack?

No. The lecture states that it still does not prevent probabilistic inference attacks and is vulnerable to similarity and composition attacks.

Q: Why can a larger k increase privacy but reduce utility?

A larger k makes each record indistinguishable from more records, making re-identification harder. But achieving larger groups generally requires more generalisation or suppression, causing more information loss and lower utility.

Topic 1 — Threats, Risks & Vulnerabilities

Exact core idea

Data security & information privacy

The lecture defines data security and information privacy around protecting people’s sensitive/personal data from unauthorised access, use or disclosure.

Data security protects data from being stolen, compromised or corrupted. Controls named in the lecture include encryption, firewalls, access controls, authentication mechanisms and regular updates.

Information privacy concerns the individual and how personal/sensitive information is accessed, used or disclosed.

Short exam distinction: Security protects the data; privacy protects the person’s information and its appropriate use/disclosure.
Properties

Security + privacy properties

ConfidentialityPrevent unauthorised access/disclosure.
IntegrityEnsure data has not been improperly modified.
AvailabilityEnsure authorised access when required.
AnonymityPrevent linking identity to an action/data. E-voting example: identity should not reveal who a person voted for.
IndistinguishabilityPrevent one person/record being singled out from the released data. AOL is the lecture example.

Property → preservation technique

PropertyLecture techniqueExam explanation
ConfidentialityEncryption / access controlsOnly users with the decryption/access key can access data.
IntegrityHashing / logsCompare hashes or inspect logged actions to verify modification.
AvailabilityBackupsBackup data can be used when original data is lost/unavailable.
Anonymity + indistinguishabilityDe-identificationExamples include k-anonymity and differential privacy.
Common exam trap: encryption is the lecture’s confidentiality control; hashing/logs are the integrity controls.

Hashing & firewall — definitions

Hashing: cryptographic technique converting a value into a hash/message digest. Even a one-character input change can produce a completely different digest. Comparing old/new digests helps verify modification.

Firewall: network security device creating a barrier between a private internal network and the Internet; it allows safe traffic and blocks dangerous traffic according to security policies/protocols.

Information-system network & data states

An IS network consists of software/computer systems, hardware, Internet, IoT devices and users sharing/receiving data.

Data at restStored, e.g. in a database.
Data in transitSent over a network/Internet.
Data in useBeing processed by software/device.

High probability

Re-identification risk

Re-identification is the risk of identifying an individual and learning more about that individual from the data. The lecture says it leads mostly to loss of anonymity, indistinguishability and confidentiality.

Lecture real-world examples: AOL anonymised search histories, Netflix training data and Massachusetts hospital discharge data.

Four re-identification attack families

AttackDefinition from lecture framingRecognition clue / example
InferenceLearning or predicting information about an individual from data.AOL: identify from some searches, then infer remaining queries.
AmplificationGain new information by linking public data with anonymised data.Medical data + public social-media profile → infer health/religious views.
DifferencingAnalyse differences in results to reveal target information.Diagnosis counts for all records vs counts excluding target.
ReconstructionReconstruct original information from synthetic, aggregate or anonymised data.Reconstruct parts of mobility trajectories from aggregate mobility data.

Auxiliary knowledge

Unique/direct identifierUnique per individual and directly identifies them; examples: email, telephone number, medical record number.
Quasi-identifier (QID)Not necessarily unique alone, but sufficiently correlated with an individual; combinations can become unique.
Background knowledgeAnything the adversary already knows about the target; example: only the target attended a clinic for a chronic condition at a known time.
AdversaryPerson, group, organisation or government intending harm through cyber activity.
Lecture statistic: approximately 87% of the US population could be uniquely identified using gender + date of birth + 5-digit ZIP in the cited Sweeney result.

Topic 2 — Privacy Risk Quantification & Evaluation

Four key elements of data privacy risk

ElementLecture meaningExample
UniquenessLikelihood that an attribute value or combination is distinguishable from other records. More unique → more re-ID risk.“Jenjira” is rarer/more identifiable than “John”.
UniformityHow similar/consistent an individual’s values are over different events/times. Repeated same values increase inference confidence.Repeated consistent searches about one medical illness.
Linkable tokensTokens containing PII usable to link directly/indirectly to an individual.Email in free-text student feedback.
CorrelationLikelihood of inferring another attribute from a known/guessed attribute; variables change in relation to one another.Part-time work correlated with late-submission penalties.

Direct identifiers vs QIDs

TypeExamplesWhy risky
Direct identifiers (DIDs)Name, email, telephone, unique medical record number.Directly and explicitly identify a person.
Quasi-identifiers (QIDs)Date of birth, gender, postcode.May not identify alone, but combined values can become unique.

Correlation details

Positive correlation: variables increase/decrease together. Negative correlation: one increases while the other decreases. Highly correlated attributes are more likely to be inferred from one another.

Cross-dataset lecture example: Dataset A has demographics (age, gender, postal code), Dataset B has browsing history (websites, time). Strong correlation can help link a person across the datasets, even if one dataset lacks a direct identifier.

Threat model — exact exam concept

A threat model defines the intents and capabilities of potential adversaries that want to compromise data/privacy. Privacy-preserving or de-identification methods should preserve privacy/security properties against the assumed model.

Model typeAdversary knowledgeRisk logic
Single attributeOne attribute, e.g. postcode.Attempts re-ID using that value.
Combination of attributesMultiple values, e.g. postcode + medical code.More information usually makes records more unique and increases estimated risk.
Direct identifierCan extract a DID from an attribute, e.g. phone number from clinical notes.Links DID to external source such as phone directory/voter database.
Alejandra/Fred scenario: assess risk if Fred knows at least two QIDs about a target or can use an external source to link a DID available in the clinic dataset.

Privacy spectrum

0.0Absolute privacy — adversary cannot re-identify or infer information.
0.25Low suspicion / low confidence of exposure.
0.50Moderate suspicion.
0.75High suspicion/high confidence, but not proved.
1.0Provably exposed — adversary can provably re-identify/infer.

Calculation Master Section

Entropy

Shannon entropy

Entropy is an information-theory measure used in the lecture to quantify privacy risk in bits. It measures uncertainty / amount of new information provided by a variable.

H(X) = − Σ p(x) log₂ p(x)
Fair binary 0.5 / 0.51 bit — maximum uncertainty/new information for two outcomes.
Deterministic 1 / 00 bits — outcome already certain.
0.6 / 0.40.971 bits — professor’s apple/orange discussion result.

Fast entropy contribution table

−p log₂p.442.360.258.1370

Entropy workflow — write these steps

  1. Define variable X according to the threat model.
  2. List each distinct value or value-combination.
  3. Count occurrences.
  4. Calculate p(x)=count/total.
  5. Substitute into −Σp(x)log₂p(x).
  6. Add all contributions.
  7. Write bits.
  8. Interpret the result relative to the other threat model/dataset.

Risk reduction

Information Gain (IG) & Relative Information Gain (RIG)

Let X be the original dataset and Y the de-identified/perturbed version.

H(X|Y)=Σ p(y)H(X|Y=y)
IG=H(X)−H(X|Y)
RIG=IG/H(X)

IG: amount that can be learned about original X from de-identified Y. Lower IG means it is harder for an adversary to infer X.

RIG: normalises IG relative to H(X) onto the lecture’s 0–1 comparison scale.

Professor’s Peter/Pete/Smith table

Complete IG/RIG worked example

Original X: Peter=30, Pete=20, Smith=50. De-identified Y: Peter+Pete → p360=50; Smith → s530=50.

Step 1 — H(X)

p(Peter)=.30, p(Pete)=.20, p(Smith)=.50
H(X)=−[.30log₂.30+.20log₂.20+.50log₂.50]
=.521+.464+.500=1.485 bits

Step 2 — H(X|Y)

For y=p360, the original may be Peter or Pete: within that group 30/50=.6 and 20/50=.4, so:

H(X|Y=p360)=−[.6log₂.6+.4log₂.4]=.971 bits
H(X|Y=s530)=0 (s530 is Smith with certainty)
p(p360)=.5, p(s530)=.5
H(X|Y)=.5(.971)+.5(0)=.486 bits

Step 3 — IG

IG=1.485−.486=.999 ≈ 1.000 bit

Step 4 — RIG

RIG=.999/1.485=.673 ≈ .67
Interpretation: the de-identified values still reveal about two-thirds of the original entropy on the RIG scale in this worked example. Lower IG/RIG would indicate less information leakage and stronger de-identification under this metric.

IG/RIG exam checklist

  1. Label X original and Y de-identified.
  2. Calculate H(X).
  3. For each Y value, calculate the remaining uncertainty about X inside that Y group.
  4. Weight by p(y) to obtain H(X|Y).
  5. IG=H(X)−H(X|Y).
  6. RIG=IG/H(X).
  7. Interpret: lower IG/RIG = harder to infer original / more risk reduction.

Topic 3 — De-identification Methods

Why privacy-preserving data sharing is needed

The lecture describes increasing sharing of individual data: shopping transactions among retailers/wholesalers/marketers for supply-chain optimisation, targeted marketing and customised products/services; and mobile-device usage collected to improve user experience. Such data can create re-identification risk, so sharing must preserve privacy/security properties.

The Topic 3 data-sharing setting assumes the data curator is trusted.

Five basic de-identification techniques

TechniqueLecture definition/example
Remove direct identifiersRemove names, Medicare numbers, email addresses, phone numbers.
AggregationPublish statistics instead of individual data; e.g. population density or disease counts.
BinningGroup values into ranges; e.g. age 0–30, 31–60, 61–100.
PerturbationModify attributes with random values/noise; lecture example adds random value from [0–20] to age.
Synthetic dataGenerate artificial data that reflects/mimics real-world distribution.
Limitation: the lecture calls these naive techniques susceptible to linkage attacks using background/auxiliary data, so de-identification does not automatically eliminate re-identification.

Provable vs probable privacy

Common models named: k-anonymity, l-diversity, t-closeness and differential privacy. The lecture states that the former three do not provide provable privacy guarantees.

Provable privacyPrivacy guarantee backed by mathematical/theoretical proof against an attack.
Probable privacyProvides privacy protection/guarantee without proof that it always holds.
Model 1

k-anonymity — complete reference

k-anonymity aims to guarantee that each individual’s information in the released dataset cannot be distinguished from at least k−1 other individuals with respect to the considered QIDs. k is user specified.

k=3 → every record looks the same as at least 2 other records on the selected QIDs.
GeneralisationReplace QID/attribute values with less specific values so at least k records share the same values; e.g. 20–25 and 25–30 → 20–30.
SuppressionRemove/mask a QID value when needed, especially where generalisation would cause excessive utility/information loss.

Larger k: stronger ambiguity/privacy, but more generalisation/suppression and therefore more information loss. The lecture says k-anonymity is better suited to large datasets because a larger k can be achieved with less relative utility loss.

Health example — what is QID vs sensitive?

QIDs: nationality, age, ZIP code. Sensitive attribute: health condition.

Without anonymisation, an external voter-registration record can link a person to a unique row and expose health condition. The lecture example links G100/Alex to R11 and infers cancer; G123/Bob can also be linked in the original data.

With k=2/k=3, exact linkage becomes more ambiguous because multiple rows share generalised QIDs.

Homogeneity attack on k-anonymity

Even if a target matches k records, privacy can fail if the sensitive attribute in that equivalence class lacks diversity.

Alex example: Alex matches 3 records and all 3 have health condition Cancer. The attacker can infer Alex has cancer → homogeneity attack.
Contrast with Bob: Bob can match R5–R8 with Cancer, Heart, Flu, Flu. Exact condition cannot be determined from that group alone.

Background attack on k-anonymity

k-anonymity does not guarantee privacy if an adversary has extra facts about the target.

Bob example: background knowledge says New Zealand people have a very low incidence of heart disease or cancer. This narrows Bob’s potential matches from four to R7/R8; both have Flu, so Flu can be inferred.
Model 2

l-diversity — complete reference

l-diversity extends k-anonymity and aims to overcome its sensitive-value homogeneity limitation. Each group/cluster sharing the same QIDs should contain at least l well-represented sensitive values, with the lecture describing approximately equal proportions.

l=3 → each equivalence class contains at least 3 diverse sensitive values.

Lecture health example uses Heart, Flu and Cancer. The anonymised table generalises QIDs into groups such as age <37 with ZIP pattern 1**6* and age ≥37 with ZIP pattern 1**5*.

Lecture claim: l-diversity protects against homogeneity and background attacks that affect k-anonymity, but it does not stop all probabilistic inference.

Similarity attack on l-diversity

l-diversity counts distinct sensitive values but does not necessarily consider that their meanings may be very similar.

Bob example: Gastric ulcer, Gastritis and Stomach cancer are three different values, yet all are stomach-related. An attacker can still infer Bob has a stomach-related disease.

Composition attack

Two k-anonymised or l-diverse datasets may be safe separately but leak more information when combined.

Hospital example: if two hospitals release anonymised research datasets and the attacker knows the target visited both, combining the releases can enable re-identification/inference. The lecture notes that the sensitive attribute is disclosed exactly.

l-diversity privacy–utility trade-off

The lecture states that l-diversity generally provides stronger privacy than k-anonymity at the cost of more information loss. Large l values are difficult on small datasets without severe utility loss; it is best suited to large datasets containing many semantically diverse sensitive values.

Model 3

t-closeness — complete reference

t-closeness aims to overcome limitations of k-anonymity and l-diversity by making the distribution of the sensitive attribute within each QID group close to the distribution of the sensitive attribute in the entire dataset.

t is user specified. The lecture uses Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD) to measure closeness.

distance(group sensitive distribution, overall sensitive distribution) ≤ t

If all groups meet the threshold, the dataset provides t-closeness.

Earth Mover’s Distance — lecture definition

The slides describe EMD as a metric for semantic similarity between strings/texts and as the minimum amount of work required to transform the first string/text into the second. Use this lecture wording if EMD is asked.

t-closeness example & result

The lecture starts from an l=3-diverse dataset and further anonymises/generalises it so the sensitive distributions are closer to the full dataset. The example reports approximately t = 0.278 for health condition.

The lecture describes reducing/restructuring groups and applying additional generalisation, which increases privacy but causes more information loss.

t-closeness protects against homogeneity/background attacks and addresses the semantic-closeness limitation that makes l-diversity vulnerable to similarity attacks.

t-closeness limitations

  • Achieving every class distribution close to the overall distribution is not trivial.
  • More generalisation may be required, reducing utility.
  • Lecture example: age becomes more generalised and ZIP masking increases from two digits to three.
  • Privacy–utility balancing is more difficult than with k-anonymity/l-diversity.
  • Like k and l, it is more practical on larger datasets.

Privacy–utility rule

Across k, l and t, stronger privacy generally requires more generalisation, suppression or distribution adjustment. That lowers information content/utility. The professor repeatedly emphasises balancing privacy protection against information loss, especially for small datasets.

l=3 means 3 rows.False. l concerns diversity of sensitive values inside a QID group.
