Answer: Approximately 67% re-identification risk / moderate suspicion under the simple encoding example.
2.60. Why is s530 conditionally certain?
Answer: s530 maps only to Smith, so H(X|Y=s530)=0.
2.61. Why is p360 conditionally uncertain?
Answer: p360 could correspond to Peter or Pete, with conditional probabilities 0.6 and 0.4.
2.62. What is H(X|Y=p360)?
Answer: Approximately 0.971 bits.
2.63. Why are IG and RIG useful for comparing de-identification methods?
Answer: They provide numerical measures of information leakage/risk reduction across methods/settings.
Topic 3 practice questions

3.1. Why is privacy-preserving data sharing needed?
Answer: Individual-level data are increasingly shared for useful applications, but re-identification/inference can compromise privacy.
3.2. What is assumed about the data curator in Topic 3?
Answer: The data curator is trusted.
3.3. Name the five basic de-identification techniques.
Answer: Remove direct identifiers, aggregation, binning, perturbation, synthetic data.
3.4. What does removing direct identifiers mean?
Answer: Delete values such as names, Medicare numbers, emails and phone numbers.
3.5. What is aggregation?
Answer: Publish group statistics instead of individual-level records.
3.6. What is binning?
Answer: Replace detailed values with ranges/groups, e.g. age 0-30, 31-60, 61-100.
3.7. What is perturbation?
Answer: Modify values with random values/noise; lecture example adds random [0-20] noise to age.
3.8. What is synthetic data?
Answer: Artificially generated data designed to mimic real-world data distribution.
3.9. Why can basic/naive de-identification still fail?
Answer: Linkage attacks can combine anonymised data with known background/auxiliary information.
3.10. Name the common de-identification models in Topic 3.
Answer: k-anonymity, l-diversity, t-closeness, differential privacy.
3.11. Which of those are stated as not providing provable privacy guarantees?
Answer: k-anonymity, l-diversity and t-closeness.
3.12. Define provable privacy.
Answer: A privacy guarantee against an attack supported by mathematical/theoretical proof.
3.13. Define probable privacy.
Answer: Privacy protection/guarantee without proof that it always holds.
3.14. Define inference attack in Topic 3.
Answer: Illegitimately learning/guessing a person's sensitive information from data.
3.15. Define k-anonymity.
Answer: Each released record is indistinguishable from at least k-1 other records with respect to selected QIDs.
3.16. What does k=3 mean?
Answer: Each record shares its QID pattern with at least 2 other records.
3.17. What is generalisation?
Answer: Replace precise QID values with less specific values to create groups of at least k matching records.
3.18. What is suppression?
Answer: Remove/mask a QID/value when needed, especially for outliers or when generalisation would create excessive utility loss.
3.19. What happens as k increases?
Answer: Privacy/ambiguity generally increases; information loss also increases.
3.20. Why does larger k increase information loss?
Answer: More generalisation/suppression is needed so each record matches a larger group.
3.21. Why is k-anonymity better suited to large datasets?
Answer: Large k can often be achieved with less relative utility loss than in small datasets.
3.22. What are the QIDs in the health example?
Answer: Nationality, age and ZIP code.
3.23. What is the sensitive attribute in the health example?
Answer: Health condition.
3.24. What happens to nationality in the k=3 table?
Answer: It is suppressed/generalised to *.
3.25. What happens to age and ZIP in the k=3 table?
Answer: Age becomes 20-30, 30-40, 40-50; ZIP becomes 130** or 148**.
3.26. What differs in the k=2 table?
Answer: Nationality is not suppressed; age and ZIP are still generalised.
3.27. Why can original R11 be linked to G100?
Answer: Nationality, age and ZIP match uniquely in the original data, identifying Alex and revealing cancer.
3.28. What does k=2 do to Bob/G123 linkage?
Answer: Makes it ambiguous between R5 and R7 in the worked discussion, so exact condition cannot be determined.
3.29. What does k=3 do to Bob/G123 linkage?
Answer: Makes it ambiguous across R5-R8, with multiple possible health conditions.
3.30. Define homogeneity attack.
Answer: Sensitive values in an equivalence class lack diversity, so the target's sensitive value can be inferred despite k-anonymity.
3.31. What is the Alex homogeneity example?
Answer: Alex matches 3 records and all 3 have Cancer; cancer can be inferred.
3.32. What is the Bob non-homogeneous example?
Answer: Bob matches R5-R8, whose conditions include Cancer, Heart and Flu, so exact condition cannot be inferred from the group alone.
3.33. Define background attack.
Answer: External knowledge narrows possible matching records/sensitive values and defeats k-anonymity.
3.34. What is the Bob background-knowledge example?
Answer: Knowing New Zealanders have low incidence of heart disease/cancer narrows Bob to R7/R8; both have Flu, so Flu is inferred.
3.35. What are the two main attacks on k-anonymity?
Answer: Homogeneity and background attacks.
3.36. Can a larger k reduce these risks?
Answer: It can reduce re-identification risk, but may require more generalisation and cause more information loss.
3.37. Define l-diversity.
Answer: Each QID group contains at least l well-represented/diverse sensitive values.
3.38. What does l=3 mean?
Answer: Each QID group should contain at least 3 diverse sensitive values.
3.39. How does l-diversity improve k-anonymity?
Answer: It adds sensitive-value diversity and addresses homogeneity/background weaknesses.
3.40. Does l-diversity stop all inference?
Answer: No. The lecture says it does not prevent probabilistic inference and is vulnerable to similarity/composition attacks.
3.41. What are the two l-diversity attacks?
Answer: Similarity and composition.
3.42. Define similarity attack.
Answer: Distinct sensitive values are semantically close, so the attacker can still infer an approximate sensitive category.
3.43. What is semantic similarity?
Answer: How similar or close the meanings of two values are.
3.44. What is the Bob similarity example?
Answer: Gastric ulcer, Gastritis and Stomach cancer are distinct but all stomach-related, so a stomach condition can be inferred.
3.45. Define composition attack.
Answer: Combining multiple anonymised releases reveals more information than each release separately.
3.46. What is the hospital composition example?
Answer: An attacker knowing a person visited two hospitals combines both anonymised datasets and infers/re-identifies sensitive attributes.
3.47. Why does composition work in the lecture example?
Answer: Sensitive attributes are disclosed exactly, allowing cross-release intersection/linkage.
3.48. How does l-diversity compare with k-anonymity on privacy?
Answer: Stronger privacy.
3.49. How does l-diversity compare on information loss?
Answer: Generally more information loss, especially with large l or small datasets.
3.50. What kind of dataset best suits l-diversity?
Answer: Large datasets with many semantically diverse sensitive values.
3.51. Define t-closeness.
Answer: Each QID group's sensitive-value distribution must remain close to the overall sensitive-value distribution within threshold t.
3.52. What does t represent?
Answer: A user-specified closeness/similarity threshold.
3.53. What metric does the lecture use for closeness?
Answer: Earth Mover's Distance (EMD).
3.54. Define EMD using the lecture wording.
Answer: A metric for semantic similarity; minimum amount of work required to transform one string/text into another.
3.55. When does a dataset satisfy t-closeness?
Answer: When every group's sensitive distribution is within threshold t of the full dataset distribution.
3.56. Which earlier attacks does the lecture say t-closeness protects against?
Answer: Homogeneity and background attacks; it also addresses semantic closeness, the key similarity limitation of l-diversity.
3.57. What t value is reported in the lecture example?
Answer: Approximately 0.278.
3.58. What structural change is made in the t-closeness example?
Answer: The l=3-diverse dataset with 3 QID groups is further anonymised into 2 groups.
3.59. How does t-closeness affect utility in the example?
Answer: More generalisation and information loss; age groups reduce from 3 to 2 and more ZIP digits are masked.
3.60. Why is t-closeness hard to apply?
Answer: It is difficult to make each class distribution close to the overall distribution while preserving useful detail.
3.61. How does the privacy-utility trade-off compare for t-closeness?
Answer: More difficult/challenging than for k-anonymity and l-diversity because stronger resistance generally requires more information loss.
3.62. What dataset size is t-closeness better suited to?
Answer: Large datasets, where stronger privacy can be achieved with less relative information loss.
3.63. Compare k, l and t in one sentence.
Answer: k hides identity among QID peers; l adds sensitive-value diversity; t additionally controls sensitive-value distributions relative to the whole dataset.
