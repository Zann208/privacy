H = -[0.5 log₂0.5 + 0.4 log₂0.4 + 0.1 log₂0.1] ≈ 1.361 bits

Alejandra - Threat Model 3: postcode + medical code

The lecture slide lists four combined values: (2015,A), (2016,A), (2016,B), (2017,C), with counts 3, 3, 3 and 1.
H = -[3(0.3 log₂0.3) + 0.1 log₂0.1] ≈ 1.895 bits

TOPIC 2 - INFORMATION GAIN AND RELATIVE INFORMATION GAIN

Risk reduction after de-identification

The effectiveness of a de-identification technique can be assessed by comparing entropy before and after de-identification, or by using Information Gain (IG) and Relative Information Gain (RIG).
X is the original dataset. Y is the perturbed/anonymised/de-identified version.
IG(X|Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y)

RIG(X|Y) = IG(X|Y) / H(X)

H(X|Y) is the conditional entropy - the remaining uncertainty about original X when the de-identified version Y is known. Lower IG means it is more difficult for an adversary to infer the original dataset from the de-identified dataset. RIG normalises IG against H(X) on a 0.0-1.0 scale.
Peter / Pete / Smith worked example

Original X | Count | De-identified Y
Peter | 30 | p360
Pete | 20 | p360
Smith | 50 | s530

H(X) = 1.4855 bits

For Y=p360, Peter/Pete remain possible with conditional probabilities 0.6 and 0.4. For Y=s530, Smith is known with certainty.
H(X|Y) = 0.4855 bits

IG = 1.4855 - 0.4855 = 1.0 bit

RIG = 1.0 / 1.4855 = 0.6732 ≈ 67%

The lecture interprets this simple encoding example as a 67% re-identification risk / moderate degree of suspicion on the privacy spectrum.
Lecture slide note: The H(X) slide contains mismatched log labels in a few table cells, but the contribution values 0.5211, 0.4644 and 0.5 and the total H(X)=1.4855 match the probabilities 0.3, 0.2 and 0.5.

TOPIC 3 - DE-IDENTIFICATION METHODS

Privacy-preserving data sharing

Individual data are increasingly shared for supply-chain optimisation, targeted marketing, customised products/services and product/user-experience improvement.
Examples in the lecture include sharing shopping transactions among retailers/wholesalers/marketers and Apple collecting mobile-device usage data.
Because data about individuals can create re-identification risk, entities collecting data need privacy-preserving data-sharing techniques.
In the Topic 3 data-sharing model, the data curator is assumed to be trusted.
Basic de-identification techniques

Technique | Definition / example
Remove direct identifiers | Remove names, Medicare numbers, email addresses, phone numbers.
Aggregation | Publish statistics rather than individual records; e.g. population density or counts of patients with different diseases.
Binning | Group detailed values into ranges; e.g. ages 0-30, 31-60, 61-100.
Perturbation | Modify attribute values using random values/noise; lecture example adds a random value from [0-20] to age.
Synthetic data | Generate and publish artificial data that reflects/mimics the distribution of real-world data.

The lecture states that these naive techniques are still susceptible to linkage attacks using known background/auxiliary data. De-identification does not automatically eliminate re-identification.
De-identification models and privacy guarantees

Common models named in the lecture are k-anonymity, l-diversity, t-closeness and differential privacy. The lecture states that k-anonymity, l-diversity and t-closeness do not provide provable privacy guarantees.
Concept | Meaning
Provable privacy | Privacy guarantee against an attack supported by mathematical/theoretical proof.
Probable privacy | Provides a privacy guarantee/protection but no proof that the guarantee always holds.
Inference attack | Technique used to illegitimately learn or make an educated guess about a person's sensitive information from a dataset/database.

TOPIC 3 - K-ANONYMITY

Definition

k-anonymity aims to ensure that each individual's information in a released dataset cannot be distinguished from at least k-1 other individuals with respect to the selected quasi-identifiers. k is user specified.
k = 3 → each record looks the same as at least 2 other records on the selected QIDs

Mechanisms

Mechanism | Meaning | Course example
Generalisation | Replace QID/attribute values with less specific values so at least k records share the same values. | Age groups 20-25 and 25-30 generalised to 20-30.
Suppression | Remove a QID/value when generalisation would create too much utility/information loss, especially for outliers. | Mask/remove a problematic QID.

Privacy-utility trade-off

Larger k gives stronger ambiguity because each record looks like more records.
Larger k requires more generalisation/suppression and therefore more information loss.
Small datasets suffer more utility loss for large k.
The lecture says k-anonymity is better suited to large datasets where a large k may be achieved without excessive information loss.
Health dataset example

The example dataset contains three QIDs - nationality, age and ZIP code - and one sensitive attribute - health condition. The dataset contains Australians and New Zealanders living in the United States.
k=3: nationality is suppressed/generalised to “*”; age is generalised into 20-30, 30-40 and 40-50; ZIP is generalised into 130** and 148** so each pattern has at least 3 records.
k=2: nationality does not need to be suppressed; age and ZIP remain generalised.
Different coloured groups in the slides represent clusters/equivalence classes sharing the same QID values.
With a smaller k, the privacy requirement can be achieved with less generalisation/suppression.
External voter-registration linkage

Without anonymisation, R11 can be linked to G100 (Alex) using nationality, age and ZIP, revealing cancer.
R5 can be linked to G123 (Bob), revealing the health condition.
With k=2, Bob/G123 can ambiguously match R5 or R7, so the condition may be cancer or flu.
With k=3, Bob/G123 can match R5-R8, so cancer, flu or heart disease remain possible.
The larger k becomes, the more ambiguous and difficult exact inference becomes.
Lecture naming note: One sentence near the end of Topic 3 Part 1 calls the all-cancer homogeneity target “Bob”; Topic 3 Part 2 uses “Alex” for the all-cancer example and “Bob” for the background-knowledge example. The Part 2 sequence is internally consistent.

TOPIC 3 - ATTACKS ON K-ANONYMITY

Homogeneity attack

Even when an external record matches k records, the sensitive attribute can still be inferred when the sensitive values in the equivalence class lack diversity.
Bob safe example: matching R5-R8 contain Cancer, Heart and Flu; the exact condition cannot be inferred.
Alex vulnerable example: Alex matches three records and all three have Cancer; cancer can be inferred.
Background attack

If the adversary has external knowledge about the target, k-anonymity may not protect against inference.
Lecture example: background knowledge states that people from New Zealand have a very low incidence of heart disease or cancer. This reduces Bob's possible matches from four to R7/R8, both of which have Flu, so Flu can be inferred.
Limitations of k-anonymity

Does not provide sufficient privacy in all cases.
Larger k reduces re-identification risk but increases information loss.
The trade-off is especially difficult for small datasets.
Best suited to larger datasets when a large k can be achieved without excessive information loss.
TOPIC 3 - L-DIVERSITY AND ATTACKS

l-diversity

l-diversity extends k-anonymity by requiring diversity in the sensitive attribute. Each cluster/group of records sharing the same QID values should contain at least l well-represented sensitive values, approximately equal in proportion according to the lecture description.
l = 3 → each QID group contains at least 3 diverse sensitive values

Health example

Each group contains at least the sensitive values Heart, Flu and Cancer.
One example group: age <37 and ZIP form 1**6*.
Second group: age ≥37 and ZIP form 1**5*.
First group has 2 records for each of Heart, Flu and Cancer.
Second group has 2 Cancer, 2 Flu and 1 Heart.
The lecture states that l-diversity protects against the homogeneity and background attacks that affect k-anonymity, but it still does not stop probabilistic inference attacks.
Similarity attack

l-diversity may have distinct sensitive values that are semantically very similar. An adversary can then infer the approximate sensitive category even though the values are technically diverse.
Lecture Bob example: Gastric ulcer, Gastritis and Stomach cancer are three distinct values, but all indicate a stomach-related disease; therefore a stomach-related condition can still be inferred.
Composition attack

Combining two k-anonymised or l-diverse datasets can reveal more information than either release alone. The lecture example uses two hospitals: an adversary who knows the target visited both can combine the releases and infer/re-identify sensitive attributes.
l-diversity trade-off

Stronger privacy than k-anonymity, but greater information loss.
May perform worse than k-anonymity on small datasets in terms of utility.
Best suited to large datasets with many semantically diverse sensitive values.
TOPIC 3 - T-CLOSENESS AND LIMITATIONS

t-closeness

t-closeness aims to overcome limitations of k-anonymity and l-diversity. t is user specified and controls how close the sensitive-attribute distribution inside each QID group is to the sensitive-attribute distribution in the entire dataset.
distance(group sensitive distribution, overall sensitive distribution) ≤ t

Closeness is measured in the lecture using Earth Mover's Distance (EMD). If every group satisfies the threshold, the dataset provides t-closeness.
Earth Mover's Distance (EMD)

The lecture defines EMD as a metric for semantic similarity between two strings/texts and describes it as the minimum amount of work required to transform the first string/text into the second.
Privacy properties

Protects against homogeneity attacks and background-knowledge attacks.
Addresses semantic closeness of sensitive values, which is a limitation of l-diversity.
Lecture example further anonymises an l=3-diverse dataset into two QID groups and reports approximately t=0.278 for health condition.
This provides stronger privacy but causes additional information loss.
Limitations and privacy-utility trade-off

Making every class distribution close to the overall distribution is not trivial.
t-closeness can require more generalisation and reduce utility.
Lecture example generalises age from 3 groups to 2 groups and masks 3 ZIP digits instead of 2.
The privacy-utility trade-off is harder to achieve than with k-anonymity and l-diversity.
Like k and l, t-closeness is more practical on large datasets when sufficient privacy can be achieved with tolerable information loss.

Security and privacy properties

Property | Meaning | Preservation method / example

k-anonymity vs l-diversity vs t-closeness

Model | Core rule | Improves | Main weakness / cost
